§2 Processing workloads · What broke, and why

One unit of work was "the whole night"

  1. Blast radius. Mandate #412 raises, and #413 through #3,000 never run.
  2. Retries are all-or-nothing. You can re-run the entire night, or nothing — and re-running debits the people who already paid.
  3. Memory grows with the data. 3,000 objects, plus 3,000 results, held for the whole run.
  4. No visibility. One log line for 3,000 financial operations. Support asks "what happened to this customer?" and you cannot answer.
The usual defence is try/except sprawl around everything — and it still doesn't give you retries.
55s. Point 2 is the one that leads into the next unit — hold it lightly here.

§2 Processing workloads · What we did

One job per mandate

@cron("0 0 * * *")

    ids = Mandate.objects.filter(active=True).values_list("id", flat=True)
    for mandate_id in ids:

@celery.task(bind=True, max_retries=3, autoretry_for=(ProviderError,))

    debit_customer(Mandate.objects.get(id=mandate_id))

We moved it onto Airflow

§3 Reliability · Why it broke

Why the same work runs twice

A queue normally hands a message to exactly one worker, and most days that is exactly what happens. But what it guarantees is at least once — not exactly once. The gap shows up at failure boundaries.

  1. A retry after a timeout. The call actually went through — you just never got the answer. This is the common one.
  2. A worker dies after the work, before the acknowledgement. The broker never heard "done", so it gives the message to someone else.
  3. A webhook is redelivered. WhatsApp, Instagram and Stripe all document this — you don't get to opt out.
  4. Two schedulers fire the same job. ← what happened to us
  5. The client sends it twice — a double-click, or its own retry.
Most days, none of this happens. That's the trap: "most days" isn't a guarantee, and here it is somebody's money.
65s. Be precise — do NOT claim queues duplicate constantly, because they don't, and someone in the room knows it. The argument is rarity vs. consequence: rare is fine for a thumbnail cache, and not fine for a debit. Next slide covers what people reach for to prevent it.

§3 Reliability · What people try first

Three ways to stop two runners colliding

Pessimistic — assume a collision. Take a lock, make everyone else wait.

with redis.lock("cron:mandates", timeout=3600):   # across machines
    process_mandates()
# or, within one database:  SELECT ... FROM mandates WHERE id = 42 FOR UPDATE

Optimistic — assume no collision. Check at the moment you write.

UPDATE mandates SET status = 'running', version = version + 1
 WHERE id = 42 AND version = 7;        -- 0 rows updated = someone beat you

Partitioning — never let two nodes want the same row. Hash the id onto a ring; each node only handles what it owns.

if ring.owner(mandate_id) == this_node:
    process(mandate_id)
75s. Define each in one line as you go — the room will have heard the words and not necessarily used them. Pessimistic: good when collisions are common, costs you waiting and deadlocks. Optimistic: good when they're rare, costs you handling the loser's retry. Consistent hashing: the ring means adding a node reshuffles a small slice of keys instead of all of them. Same trick as sharding a cache. Do NOT oversell any of them — the next slide is why none of them finish the job.

§3 Reliability · Why that isn't enough

All three narrow the window. None of them close it.

ApproachWhere it still lets you down
Distributed lock The holder stalls — a GC pause, a slow disk, a paused container — and the TTL expires while the work is still running. Now there are two, and neither knows.
Optimistic locking Protects the row you version. It doesn't protect the debit you already sent to the payment provider.
Consistent hashing Ownership moves whenever a node joins or leaves. During that shuffle, two nodes can both believe they own the same key.
Every one of these makes a second run less likely. None of them makes a second run safe. That is a different problem, and it needs a different tool.
60s. This is the hinge of the section. Land the last line and pause — the next slide is the answer. If asked "so are locks useless?" — no. Use them to avoid wasted work and contention. Just don't use them as your correctness guarantee.

§3 Reliability · The concept

Idempotency, and where the key comes from

Idempotent — running it twice leaves the same result as running it once.

key = str(uuid4())                     # new key every attempt — useless
key = f"{mandate_id}:{run_date}"       # same key every attempt

Derive the key from the work, not from the attempt.

Good keys

  • mandate_id + run_date
  • order_id
  • the provider's message id

Bad keys

  • anything with a timestamp
  • a fresh UUID
  • the hostname or retry count
Some work is idempotent for free: SET balance = 5000, an upsert, a delete by id. Never free: balance = balance + 500, append, send, charge.
60s. Webhooks hand you a free key — the provider's message id. Most immediately useful slide in the talk.

§3 Reliability · What broke, and why

The API got slow, and we made it worse

One afternoon the classification service started responding slowly. Not down — just slow. Our retry code looked like this:

for attempt in range(5):
    try:
        return classify(url)
    except Exception:
        time.sleep(1)          # wait exactly one second, then try again
t+0s   the API slows down
t+1s   every worker retries at the same instant  ──▶ more load, slower still
t+2s   every worker retries at the same instant  ──▶ it starts returning errors
t+3s   every worker retries at the same instant  ──▶ ...
  • Every worker failed at roughly the same moment, waited the same fixed second, and came back together — so we multiplied the load on a service that was already struggling.
  • Meanwhile each worker sat blocked inside sleep, so our own queue backed up behind an outage we were helping cause.

§3 Reliability · The concept

An open circuit is a chance to go elsewhere

Failing fast is good. Failing over is better — if the work can be done by somebody else.

class PaymentProvider(Protocol):
    def charge(self, amount: int, ref: str) -> ChargeResult: ...

def charge(amount, ref):
    for provider in PROVIDERS:                  # primary, then fallback
        if breaker.is_open(provider.name):
            continue                            # known down — skip it
        result = provider.charge(amount, ref)
        record(ref, provider=provider.name, external_id=result.id)
        return result
    raise AllProvidersDown

This only works if you built for it before the outage.

55s. Very common in Nigerian fintech — more than one payment provider is normal, not exotic. The room will recognise this immediately.

§3 Reliability · The concept

What failover demands of your code

  1. One interface, many adapters. The caller must not know or care which provider answered. The moment business logic says if provider == "x", failover has stopped being switchable.
  2. Normalise everything on the way back in. Same result shape, same status names, same error categories. Two providers will not agree on what "pending" means — that translation is your job, at the edge.
  3. Record who processed what. Store the provider name and their reference id against the transaction.
That last one is not bookkeeping. Reconciliation, refunds and disputes all go back to the provider that actually moved the money — and the two of them have completely different id spaces. Your idempotency key stays yours; theirs is just something you store alongside it.
60s. Point 3 links straight back to idempotency and to the ledger in the next section. Say it: switching providers must never mean charging twice.

§4 Databases · Why it broke

The check was correct. It wasn't atomic.

   request A                request B
   ─────────                ─────────
   read balance  5000
                            read balance  5000
   check 5000 >= 4200  ok
                            check 5000 >= 4200  ok
   write balance  800
                            write balance  −4200

Between the read and the write, someone else read the same number. Both requests were individually correct.

This is a race condition: the result depends on the order two things happen to interleave.

§4 Databases · What we did

Make the check and the write one operation

-- check and write in a single statement
UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - :amt
 WHERE id = :uid AND balance >= :amt;     -- 0 rows updated = declined
# or hold the row while you decide
acct = db.query(Account).filter_by(id=uid).with_for_update().one()
-- and a backstop the application cannot forget
ALTER TABLE accounts ADD CONSTRAINT bal_nonneg CHECK (balance >= 0);

The single statement is the right default. The constraint costs nothing and saves you when someone adds a new code path next year.

§4 Databases · The concept

The risky part is a balance you mutate

balance = balance - amount is a read-modify-write — the race from the last slide, built into your schema. And it keeps what the balance is while losing how it got there.

One answer: make the entries the source of truth, and derive the number.

INSERT INTO ledger (account_id, amount, reason, idem_key)
VALUES (42, -4200, 'withdrawal', 'wd:918:2026-08-21');

SELECT SUM(amount) FROM ledger WHERE account_id = 42;
  • Nothing is read-modify-written, so nothing is lost
  • idem_key is unique, so replays are harmless
  • You get the audit trail for free
55s. Aside: a logistics app I worked on incremented and decremented counters on every action — races everywhere, same cause. Do NOT present this as a law. The next slide is the cost.

§4 Databases · The trade-off

But summing it every time isn't free

Every read now scans every entry that account has ever had. At a few hundred rows, fine. At a few million — on your hottest endpoint, on every page load — that is a real cost, and "just derive it" stops being good advice.

So most systems keep both: entries as the truth, a stored number for speed.

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO ledger (account_id, amount, reason, idem_key) VALUES (...);
  UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + :amt WHERE id = 42;   -- same transaction
COMMIT;

-- or snapshot periodically, and read: snapshot + entries since
INSERT INTO balance_snapshots (account_id, as_of_entry_id, balance) VALUES (...);
The rule worth keeping isn't "never store it". It's: store it if you need the speed — but make sure you can always recompute it from the entries, and run something that checks the two still agree.
60s. This is the honest version. A precomputed balance is a cache, and it obeys cache rules: it can drift, so it needs a reconciliation job. Links forward to §5 — that reconciliation check is exactly the kind of invariant worth alerting on.

§4 Databases · The concept

What belongs inside a transaction

Locked for someone else's outage

with db.begin():
    order.status = "paid"
    provider.charge(...)   # 200ms… or 30s
    send_email(...)

Locked for microseconds

with db.begin():
    order.status = "paid"
    db.add(Outbox(
        topic="order.paid",
        payload={"id": order.id}))

A transaction is all-or-nothing — and it holds locks the whole time. Never hold one across a network call you don't control.

Writing the intent in the same transaction, then delivering it separately, is the outbox. One atomic write; the email or the event goes out after.

60s. We stored every notification so we could replay failures — that was an outbox without the name. And a replay is a second execution, so it needs §3's key. End of §4 unit 5 around 23:00.

§4 Databases · The concept

So who actually sends it?

The outbox row is just a row. Something has to pick it up — a relay: a small worker doing one thing, on a loop.

SELECT id, topic, payload FROM outbox
 WHERE published_at IS NULL
 ORDER BY id
 LIMIT 100
 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;      -- so several relays can run without fighting
for row in batch:
    broker.publish(row.topic, row.payload)     # publish first
    mark_published(row.id)                     # then mark it done

Crash between those two lines and it publishes again — which is at-least-once, and fine, because the consumer already has an idempotency key.

Not a database trigger. A trigger runs inside your transaction and can't safely make a network call — you'd be holding a lock open across the broker. At larger scale, swap polling for change data capture: Debezium tails Postgres' replication log and publishes for you. Lower latency, more moving parts.
70s. This is the slide people actually need — everyone says "outbox" and nobody shows the relay. Order matters: publish THEN mark. The other way round loses events instead of duplicating them, and duplicates are the failure you already handle. SKIP LOCKED callback to the tool table earlier.

§4 Databases · What we nearly did

What a migration file usually looks like

-- migrations/0042_add_currency.sql        orders: 2.4 million rows

ALTER TABLE orders ADD COLUMN currency text;                -- instant

UPDATE orders SET currency = 'NGN' WHERE currency IS NULL;  -- locks 2.4M rows,
                                                            -- in one transaction

ALTER TABLE orders ALTER COLUMN currency SET NOT NULL;      -- full table scan,
                                                            -- blocks reads AND writes

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX ON orders (reference);                  -- blocks writes for
                                                            -- the whole build

Four ordinary lines. One is instant. The other three hold your production database while the deploy sits there waiting.

60s. Everyone has written this file. That's the point — it isn't reckless code, it's the normal way to write a migration, and it stops being safe at some row count. Nuance if asked: ADD COLUMN with a CONSTANT default is instant on Postgres 11+. A volatile default like gen_random_uuid() still rewrites every row.

§4 Databases · Why it breaks things

A slow migration doesn't just run slowly

  • It's one transaction. Every row that UPDATE touches stays locked until the entire migration commits — not until that statement finishes.
  • Some of it blocks readers too. SET NOT NULL and table rewrites take ACCESS EXCLUSIVE: no writes, and no reads either.
  • Locks queue, and the queue is the real killer. Your migration waits behind whatever long query is already running — and every request that arrives after it waits behind your migration.
SET lock_timeout = '3s';   -- fail fast instead of building a queue behind you
Migrations change structure, and they should be quick. Moving data is a separate job — one that runs in batches, can be paused, and can be resumed. Don't put a two-million-row UPDATE in a file your deploy is waiting on.
65s. The queue point is the one people haven't thought about: a migration that only needs 200ms can still stall the site for a minute, because of what it got stuck behind and what piled up behind it. Gentler NOT NULL if asked: ADD CONSTRAINT ... CHECK (col IS NOT NULL) NOT VALID, then VALIDATE CONSTRAINT — that takes a much weaker lock.

§4 Databases · What we did

A backfill script, run in batches

last_id = load_checkpoint()                          # resumable
while True:
    rows = (db.query(Old).filter(Old.id > last_id)     # keyset, not OFFSET
              .order_by(Old.id).limit(1000).all())     # bounded memory
    if not rows:
        break
    with db.begin():                                   # short transaction
        db.execute(insert(New), [map_row(r) for r in rows])
    last_id = rows[-1].id
    save_checkpoint(last_id)                           # safe to restart
    log.info("backfill.progress", extra={"last_id": last_id})
    time.sleep(0.1)                                    # leave room for users
Why id > last_id and not OFFSET: LIMIT 1000 OFFSET 90000 makes Postgres fetch and throw away 90,000 rows to hand you 1,000 — so every batch is slower than the one before it. Keyset paging is an index seek: batch 900 costs the same as batch 1.

Nine lines, and every one of them is a pattern from earlier in this talk.

65s. Point at each comment: bounded memory (unit 2), safe restart (unit 3), short transaction (unit 5), visible progress (unit 7). Say it isn't a coincidence.

§4 Databases · The concept

Never move data while people are using it

1. EXPAND       add the new column or table — nothing reads it yet
2. DUAL-WRITE   write both, keep reading the old one
3. BACKFILL     in batches, resumable, off-peak
4. CONTRACT     switch reads over, then drop the old one

Four deploys instead of one. Each step is independently reversible, and at no point is there a version of the app that can't read the data.

For indexes, CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY builds without blocking writes. It's slower, it can't run inside a transaction, and a failure leaves an INVALID index you have to drop by hand.
50s. CUT CANDIDATE if running long — the script slide is the keeper.

§5 Observability · The situation

An event system made of database triggers

   database row changes
            │
            ▼
   cloud function wakes up  ──▶ diff old vs new
            │                        │
            ▼                        ▼
   guess what happened  ──▶  decide what to do next

A logistics product — people booking trucks to move shipments from the port. We had no message bus. Instead, functions watched the database for changes and worked out what had happened by comparing snapshots.

45s.

§5 Observability · What it cost us

Three things we could never do

  1. Track anything. There was no "booking confirmed" anywhere in the system — only a row that used to look one way and now looks another. You cannot count, search or alert on something that was never given a name.
  2. Retry anything. When a function failed, there was nothing to replay. The database change had already happened; the reaction was simply lost, and lost quietly.
  3. Add anything safely. A new feature meant another function diffing the same rows, re-deriving the same meaning — and hoping it didn't collide with the two already doing it.
Debugging meant reconstructing what somebody had intended, from data that had since changed again.
55s. Own it as a mistake — "this is something we built, and it was the wrong call." The room trusts that far more than a list of best practices.

§5 Observability · What we should have done

Name the event. Publish it. Consume it.

          booking confirmed
                  │
                  ▼
        ┌───────────────────┐
        │ booking.confirmed │   ← an event, with a name
        └───────────────────┘
                  │
        ┌─────────┼─────────┬──────────┐
        ▼         ▼         ▼          ▼
     notify    invoice   analytics   audit log
  • Easier to track. The name is searchable, countable, alertable. "How many bookings were confirmed today" becomes a query instead of an archaeology project.
  • Easier to retry. The event is a record. A consumer failed? Replay it — which is exactly why it needs the idempotency key from earlier.
  • Easier to build. A new feature is a new consumer. Nothing that already works has to be touched.
60s. Land this: we already had the outbox from the last section available to us. An outbox row IS a named event. We just never thought of it that way.

§5 Observability · The concept

"The dashboard says it's fine"

A customer says the app is slow. You open the dashboard. Average response time: 80ms. Green across the board. You tell them it looks fine on your end.

You are both telling the truth.

1,000 requests

    999  took     50 ms
      1  took     30 s
   ─────────────────────
   mean          80 ms      ← what the dashboard showed you

The average is real. It just describes a request that never happened to anybody.

50s. Tell it as the conversation — customer says slow, dashboard says fine. Everyone in the room has been on one side of it. One outlier in a thousand moved the mean from 50ms to 80ms and stayed invisible.

§5 Observability · The concept

Why the slow 1% isn't 1% of your users

Line every request up from fastest to slowest:

p50    the middle one          — half your requests were faster
p99    99 out of 100 were faster than this
p99.9  999 out of 1,000 were faster than this

Now: one page load in your app makes 30 API calls. For that page to feel fast, all 30 have to land in the quick 99%.

0.99 ^ 30  =  0.74

   →  26% of page loads hit the slow path.
   →  roughly 1 in 4.

p99 is not your unluckiest customer. It is a quarter of your traffic. Alert on it.

60s. This is the number that makes the point — say it slowly. The intuition people have is "p99 = 1% of users, so who cares". The maths says the opposite as soon as one page makes many calls. If asked: 10 calls -> 10%, 30 calls -> 26%, 50 calls -> 40%.

§5 Observability · The concept

Four things worth instrumenting

  1. Queue depth and oldest-message age. The earliest signal that work is arriving faster than you drain it. Latency alerts fire minutes later.
  2. p99 latency per endpoint. A global p99 hides one bad route.
  3. Retry and failure rate per dependency. Rising retries mean a circuit breaker is about to matter.
  4. Slow queries by total time, not average. A query running 100,000 times at 20ms costs more than the 2-second one you're staring at.
And whatever must never be true — alert on it. SELECT count(*) FROM accounts WHERE balance < 0 should be a monitor, not something a customer tells you.
